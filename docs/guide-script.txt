What this event is.

A one-day AI hackathon where multidisciplinary teams build working prototypes for real Connecticut civic challenges: things like local news deserts, food access, transit gaps, and cost of living. Teams get access to mentors, AI compute resources, and a full day of hands-on building, ending in five-minute presentations and audience voting.

No prior hackathon experience is required. Beginners and experienced builders are equally welcome.

Challenges.

Challenge 1: Where Help Doesn't Reach. Theme: community building and impact, civic engagement.

United Way's ALICE report identifies which Connecticut towns have the highest share of households that earn above the poverty line but still can't cover basic costs. ALICE stands for Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed. United Way 211 service data and CTData dot org show where social services are physically located.

Teams build a tool that overlays ALICE population density against the location of assistance resources, like 211 services, food pantries, and financial counseling, to surface towns or neighborhoods underserved relative to need. Output could be a map, a gap-scoring tool, or a plain-language report a nonprofit could hand to a funder.

Data sources: United Way ALICE Report, public PDF and dataset by town; CTData dot org town-level demographics; 211 CT service locator, if scrapable or public. Additional useful data: the CT Data Collaborative repositories on GitHub.

Six-hour feasibility: static or lightly interactive map or dashboard using AI to summarize gaps in plain language. No live data pipeline needed.

AI angle: cluster towns by need profile, generate a written gap summary per region, or build a simple chat interface for exploring the data.

Challenge 2: The Last Mile of Food Access. Theme: environment, health, livability.

Food insecurity in Connecticut is well documented, with Feeding America and Foodshare service area data and USDA food desert maps, but the practical barrier for many families is proximity and transit, not just availability.

Teams build a tool that helps a resident or caseworker find the nearest realistic food resource, like a pantry, mobile market, or SNAP-accepting grocer, accounting for whether they have a car, rely on public transit, or are walking distance. Bonus: match hours of operation to a person's schedule.

Data sources: Feeding America and Foodshare public service area data; USDA food desert and food access research atlas; CTtransit public GTFS route data; public pantry directories.

Six-hour feasibility: a location-input tool returning ranked, realistic options. Can be built as a simple web app or a well-designed AI chat assistant with the data pre-loaded.

AI angle: natural language input, like "I don't have a car, I'm near X"; AI-generated plain-language directions and summary; or predictive "likely to be open or stocked" scoring if hours data is inconsistent.

Challenge 3: Student Life Beyond the Classroom. Theme: human connection, student life.

College and high school students in Connecticut often don't know what's available to them locally: mental health resources, free events, tutoring, internships, food assistance on campus.

Teams build a tool aggregating public resources for a specific student population, for example UConn Hartford, or Connecticut high schoolers generally, into one searchable or conversational interface.

Data sources: university public resource pages; 211 CT; local nonprofit event listings, like Luma and Eventbrite public pages; public-edsight dot ct dot gov.

Six-hour feasibility: scope to one campus or school district. Manually curate a starter dataset rather than scraping broadly.

AI angle: conversational resource-finder, like "I'm stressed about money, what's available to me"; auto-categorization of scattered resources.

Challenge 4: CTData dot org, Made Conversational. Theme: community building and impact, CTData dot org.

CTData dot org hosts extensive town- and state-level datasets, but the interface requires knowing what you're looking for. Most residents, journalists, and small nonprofits don't have the data literacy to use it well.

Teams build a natural-language interface over a subset of CTData dot org's public datasets, letting a user ask plain questions and get a chart, table, or plain-language answer.

Data sources: CTData dot org public datasets, CSV or API where available.

Six-hour feasibility: scope to two or three datasets, for example population, income, education, rather than the full catalog.

AI angle: this challenge is the most directly AI-native: natural language query over structured data, AI-generated chart and summary output.

What your team is expected to produce. By the end of the day, every team presents: a clearly articulated problem and the specific person it affects; a proposed solution with a scope realistic for one day; supporting research: user needs, existing alternatives, or relevant data; a working prototype, mockup, AI workflow, or credible product demonstration; a five-minute presentation covering all of the above, plus impact and next steps.

Presentation format, five minutes per team. Problem and User, 45 seconds: what problem, for whom? Research and Insight, 45 seconds: what evidence shaped your solution? Solution and Demonstration, two minutes: show the concept, workflow, or prototype. Impact and Differentiation, one minute: why is it useful or distinctive? Next Step, 30 seconds: what would you build, test, or validate next?

A slide template link will be added once created.

How you'll be evaluated. Audience voting, plus structured judge feedback on: problem clarity, meaning is the problem specific, real, and well-defined; solution quality, meaning is the solution original, feasible, and well-scoped; AI effectiveness, meaning is AI used in a meaningful, intentional way; research and evidence, meaning is there user, market, or data evidence behind the approach; impact potential, meaning could this make a real difference for real people; and presentation, meaning is the story clear, compelling, and well delivered.

FAQ.

Intellectual property and ownership. Do participants retain ownership of ideas, research, designs, code, and other materials they create before and during the hackathon? Neither AI Collective, nor its hosts, nor partners or sponsors, nor the organizers make any claim for any IP created for the event. It is up to team participants to decide on IP ownership going forward.

Does the organizer or any sponsor or partner receive a license or other rights to participant submissions? There are no licensing claims of any kind on materials created.

Will presentations or demonstrations be recorded, photographed, published, or otherwise shared publicly? There are no plans to stream the event. We don't plan to record the presentations. Photos and short videos will be taken for promotional purposes. Your attendance represents an opt-in to those photos.

Participants and audience may take photos and videos during the event. This is a public event. Neither the AI Collective nor its hosts has any control over that.

Is there a participant agreement or written intellectual-property policy to review? Refer to the IP rules section of this document.

Presentation format. Is the five-minute presentation inclusive of the product demonstration? Yes. There is a total of five minutes per team to present and demo. You and your team will decide how to split time between presentation and demo.

Will there be a separate question-and-answer period? Yes. There is a separate Q and A and feedback session, two to three minutes in length, depending on the schedule that evening.

May more than one team member participate, or should one person present? That is up to you, though organizers have found that transition time between presenters could eat up valuable seconds.

Are slides required, and must they be submitted in advance or in a particular format? Slides are encouraged, and there is a template in the presentation folder of the Playbook.

What audiovisual equipment and internet access will be available? The University of Connecticut has free public WiFi and an audio and video system.

Judging and recognition. There will be swag and prizes throughout the event. Will there be one overall winner or first, second, and third place recognition? The top three will be recognized. The top vote-getter is the only one with actual prizes. Will winners be selected by judges, audience voting, or a combination? Judges only. Are there prizes, awards, or other forms of recognition? Free mentoring from a startup accelerator will be provided to the top team, if they plan to proceed. There will be other prizes and swag for participants and winners. More details coming soon. Are there any tie-breaking criteria beyond the judging rubric? Judges will determine the final rankings.

What happens after the event. Will teams receive written or verbal feedback from the judges? Judges' scores will be provided. Additional feedback is at the discretion of the judges. Are there opportunities for continued mentorship, incubation, introductions, or follow-up support? There will be mentoring for the winning team. All participants are invited to AI Collective Hartford events, which also include Vibin' Out sessions, Demo Nights, and monthly talks, plus other events where you can meet and connect with mentors and AI practitioners. Will any of the projects be featured through UConn, the AI Collective, or event partners? All participants are invited to subsequent AI Collective demo days across the state: Stamford, Hartford, and New Haven.

IP Rules. AI Collective Hackathon core intellectual property rules.

Participant ownership: you and your team own one hundred percent of the intellectual property you create.

No organizer claims: event organizers, sponsors, and partners do not take ownership or commercial exploitation rights over your project.

Open source and portfolios: you are free to choose any license for your project, publish it in public repositories, and use it in your personal CV or portfolio.

Original work warranty: you must warrant that your submission is your original work and does not infringe on any third-party patents, copyrights, or trademarks.

Public presentation: understand that you are making a public presentation and that the organizers have no control over the people in the audience. If you truly have some protectable IP, simply do not mention that in your presentation.
